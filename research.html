Dirty Air, Shifting Roles: The Gendered Effects of Pollution Exposure

with Aina Puig

Abstract

Air pollution is an increasingly important constraint on labor supply and household well-being, yet little is known about how it shapes intra-household time allocation. This paper examines the effect of pollution on spousal time use in rural Bangladesh, a highly exposed and understudied setting. Matching three waves of panel household survey data to satellite measures of air pollution, we instrument exposure with thermal inversions, enabling causal identification of the effect of pollution on time allocation.

Pollution shocks reallocate time within households along gendered lines rather than uniformly reducing labor supply. Women move out of outdoor market work and into indoor home-based textile production, reduce time in unpaid work, and spend more time indoors, including in leisure. Men’s market work does not change significantly on average, but they spend less time on unpaid housework. These responses are shaped by household endowments rather than care burdens. Among men in asset-rich households, pollution shifts time out of outdoor and agricultural work and toward indoor wage employment, relative to men in poorer households. Women’s retreat from outdoor work is concentrated among those with no schooling, and their move into home-based textile work is largest in poorer households. Women’s responses do not vary with the number of dependents, and unpaid care time is unaffected for either spouse, indicating that the reallocation is not driven by caregiving demands. Weak-instrument-robust inference confirms the shift of women’s time into home-based textile work; men’s estimates are less precise. These results show that pollution reshapes how spouses divide their time, with important implications for household welfare and policy.
